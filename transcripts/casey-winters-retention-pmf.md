## Why Retention is the Only Real Health Metric

Lenny Rachitsky: Casey Winters, former Chief Product Officer at Eventbrite and growth lead at Pinterest and Grubhub, joins us today. Casey, when a founder asks you if they have product-market fit, what is the single graph you ask to see?

Casey Winters: The cohort retention curve. Nothing else matters if retention curves go to zero. 
Most founders show me cumulative signups or Gross Merchandise Value (GMV) charts pointing up and to the right. That is vanity. If your retention curves are angling down toward zero on month 6, 12, or 24, your business is a leaky bucket. Growth will inevitably hit a plateau where user churn matches new user acquisition, and your marketing dollars will burn away.

True Product-Market Fit looks like a curve that drops initially—as casual or non-target visitors churn—and then flattens out into a horizontal line parallel to the baseline. Even if that horizontal plateau is at 20% or 30%, that stable baseline represents a core group of users receiving enduring, recurring value.

## The Three Phases of Retention

Lenny Rachitsky: How do you systematically improve retention across the user journey?

Casey Winters: Break retention into three distinct phases:
1. Setup and Activation (Day 0 to Day 7): This is where 60% to 80% of churn occurs. The user came with high intent, but friction, confusing UI, or delayed time-to-value caused them to abandon. Fix activation by streamlining the onboarding flow to deliver the single core promise immediately.
2. Habit Formation (Week 2 to Month 2): Building trigger-action-reward loops. For Grubhub, the trigger was hunger on Friday night; for Pinterest, it was browsing inspiration on Sunday afternoon. You must identify what external and internal triggers bring the user back without needing paid push notifications.
3. Long-term Value and Resurrection: For mature users, retention is sustained through switching costs, data accumulation (stored playlists, saved boards, workspace history), and team collaboration.

## Feature Traps vs. Core Loop Enhancement

Lenny Rachitsky: What is the most dangerous mistake product teams make when trying to improve retention?

Casey Winters: Building more peripheral features. When retention is lagging, engineering teams often panic and ship 10 new features that only 2% of the user base ever touches. Instead of bolting on extra features, you need to dissect why the core 80% did not reach the primary value proposition. Usually the issue is not lack of functionality, but onboarding friction, lack of clarity, or pursuing the wrong target persona.
